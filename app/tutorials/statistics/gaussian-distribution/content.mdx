# Gaussian Distribution

The **Gaussian** (or **normal**) distribution is the most important distribution in
statistics. It shows up everywhere: measurement errors, heights of people, pixel
noise, gradient updates in neural networks.

## Where does the bell curve come from?

Before we write down any formula, let's start with raw data. Imagine you
measure something — say the height of 50 random people — and tally the results
into bins. That tally is a **histogram**: the height of each bar tells you how
many measurements landed in that range.

Try it below. Start with 50 samples, then crank it up to 500 or 5,000. As the
sample count grows and the bins get narrower, the jagged histogram silhouette
smooths out into a familiar shape — the **bell curve**. Toggle "Show PDF curve"
to see how closely the histogram tracks the theoretical Gaussian density.

The bell curve is not something we impose on data — it **emerges** from it.
The Gaussian PDF is simply the mathematical function that describes this
limiting shape. Let's write it down.

## The equation

A continuous random variable $X$ follows a Gaussian distribution $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$
if its **probability density function** (PDF) is:

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \, \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$

Two parameters control everything:

- **$\mu$ (mu)** — the **mean**, the center of the bell curve. Shifting $\mu$
  slides the entire curve left or right without changing its shape.
- **$\sigma$ (sigma)** — the **standard deviation**, a measure of spread. A
  small $\sigma$ gives a tall, narrow peak; a large $\sigma$ gives a short, wide
  one. The total area under the curve is always $1$.
