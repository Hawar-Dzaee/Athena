# LARS: Layer-wise Adaptive Rate Scaling

Training with large batches should be simple — more samples per step means
less noise and faster wall-clock convergence. In practice, naively scaling the
batch size past a few thousand causes accuracy to collapse. The learning rate
that works for one layer is wrong for another.

[LARS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03888) (You, Gitenthal & Darrell, 2017)
diagnoses the problem as a **norm mismatch**: different layers have wildly
different ratios of weight magnitude to gradient magnitude. A single global
learning rate can't account for that.

## The problem with a flat learning rate

In standard SGD the update rule is the same everywhere:

$$
w^l \leftarrow w^l - \eta \, \nabla L(w^l)
$$

Consider two layers:

- **Early conv layer**: large weights ($\|w\| = 12$), tiny gradients
  ($\|\nabla L\| = 0.02$). The update $\eta \cdot 0.02$ barely nudges the
  weights — training stalls.
- **Final FC layer**: small weights ($\|w\| = 1.4$), large gradients
  ($\|\nabla L\| = 3.1$). The update $\eta \cdot 3.1$ overshoots — training
  destabilizes.

Cranking $\eta$ up helps the first layer but blows up the second.
Cranking it down stabilizes the second but starves the first.

## The LARS rule

LARS replaces the single $\eta$ with a **per-layer local rate** $\lambda^l$:

$$
\lambda^l = \eta \;\cdot\; \varphi \;\cdot\;
\frac{\|w^l\|}{\|\nabla L(w^l)\| + \beta\,\|w^l\|}
$$

where:

- $\eta$ is the global (base) learning rate
- $\varphi$ is the **trust coefficient** — a small scalar (typically 0.001–0.02)
  that controls how aggressively the ratio is applied
- $\|w^l\|$ is the L2 norm of the weights in layer $l$
- $\|\nabla L(w^l)\|$ is the L2 norm of the gradients in layer $l$
- $\beta$ is weight decay

The update becomes:

$$
w^l \leftarrow w^l - \lambda^l \, \nabla L(w^l)
$$

The ratio $\|w\| / \|\nabla L\|$ is the key insight: it normalizes the step
size relative to the layer's own scale. Layers where gradients are small
compared to weights get amplified; layers where gradients dominate get damped.

## Why this unlocks large-batch training

Large batches reduce gradient noise, but they also amplify the norm mismatch
between layers. With SGD, there's no learning rate that works for all layers
simultaneously at batch sizes of 8K, 16K, or 32K.

LARS decouples the layers: each one gets a step size proportional to its own
weight/gradient ratio. The paper trains ResNet-50 on ImageNet with **batch
size 32K** in under an hour — matching the accuracy of small-batch training.

## Key takeaways

- **One learning rate doesn't fit all layers.** Weight and gradient norms vary
  by orders of magnitude across a network.
- **LARS scales each layer's LR by $\|w\| / \|\nabla L\|$.** This makes the
  update proportional to the layer's own scale, not a global constant.
- **The trust coefficient $\varphi$ is the safety valve.** It keeps the
  per-layer scaling conservative enough to remain stable.
- **Large-batch training is the payoff.** LARS (and its successor LAMB for
  Adam-style optimizers) made it practical to train at batch sizes of 32K+
  without accuracy degradation.
