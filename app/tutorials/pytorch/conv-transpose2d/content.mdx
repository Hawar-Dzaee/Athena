# ConvTranspose2d

A regular `Conv2d` slides a kernel over the input and produces a **smaller**
output. `nn.ConvTranspose2d` goes the other direction: it takes a small
feature map and produces a **larger** one — an **upsampling** operation.

The mechanism: for each input pixel with value $x_{ij}$, place a copy of the
kernel $W$ scaled by $x_{ij}$ at stride-spaced positions in the output, then
sum all overlapping contributions:

$$
\text{out}(o_r, o_c) \;=\! \sum_{\substack{i,\,j \\ k_r = o_r - i \cdot s + p \\ k_c = o_c - j \cdot s + p}} x_{ij} \;\cdot\; W(k_r, k_c)
$$

The output spatial size follows a simple formula — notice it is the
_reverse_ of the `Conv2d` output-size formula:

$$
H_{\text{out}} = (H_{\text{in}} - 1) \times s - 2p + k
$$

This is the standard upsampling layer in decoder networks: U-Net skip
connections, DCGAN generators, semantic segmentation heads, and
super-resolution models all rely on `ConvTranspose2d` to go from compressed
features back to spatial detail.

## Why "transposed"?

If you unroll a regular convolution as a matrix multiply
$\mathbf{y} = C\,\mathbf{x}$, then the transposed convolution computes
$C^\top \mathbf{y}$ — the same weight matrix, transposed. It is **not** an
inverse: $C^\top \neq C^{-1}$. The name "deconvolution" appears in some
older papers but is technically a misnomer.

The practical takeaway: `ConvTranspose2d` shares its weights with a
hypothetical `Conv2d` of the same kernel, stride, and padding, but reverses
the spatial direction — downsampling becomes upsampling.
