# Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)

The DFT takes $N$ samples and decomposes them into $N$ frequency bins.
Where the [continuous Fourier transform](/tutorials/math/fourier-transform) integrates over all time, the DFT sums over finite samples:

$$
X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N{-}1} x[n] \, e^{-i\,2\pi k n / N}
\qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, N{-}1
$$

Expanding Euler's formula splits each bin into real and imaginary parts — the same decomposition as the continuous case, but with sums instead of integrals:

$$
X[k]
= \underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^{N{-}1} x[n]\cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{2\pi k n}{N}\bigr)}_{\text{Real part}}
\;-\; i\underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^{N{-}1} x[n]\sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{2\pi k n}{N}\bigr)}_{\text{Imaginary part}}
$$

The magnitude tells us how strongly frequency bin $k$ is present:

$$
|X[k]| = \sqrt{\operatorname{Re}^2 + \operatorname{Im}^2}
$$

## Explore it

Pick a signal, choose a frequency bin $k$, and watch the decomposition sample by sample.
Each **stem** is one term of the sum — when positive and negative terms cancel, that frequency is weak.
When they reinforce, that frequency is strong.

### What to try

- **cos(2π·3n/N)** — Set $k = 3$ and watch every stem point the same way: perfect reinforcement. Move $k$ away and watch the stems scatter and cancel.

- **Two cosines** — The spectrum shows peaks at $k = 2$ and $k = 7$. The second peak is half the height, matching the 0.5 coefficient.

- **Impulse δ[n]** — Only $x[0] = 1$, so each sum has just one nonzero term. The spectrum is perfectly flat — every frequency is equally present in an impulse.

- **Rectangular window** — Sharp edges create a sinc-like pattern with sidelobes. Try increasing $N$ and watch the lobes tighten.

- **Exponential decay** — Smooth roll-off gives a smooth spectrum. No sharp edges means no ringing.
