# The Fourier Transform

The Fourier transform decomposes a signal $f(t)$ into its constituent frequencies.
At its heart is Euler's formula:

$$
e^{-i\omega t} = \cos(\omega t) - i\sin(\omega t)
$$

The transform "probes" a signal at frequency $\omega$ by multiplying and integrating:

$$
F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) \, e^{-i\omega t} \, dt
$$

Expanding Euler's formula splits this into a **real** and **imaginary** part:

$$
F(\omega)
= \underbrace{\int f(t)\cos(\omega t)\,dt}_{\text{Real part}}
\;-\; i\underbrace{\int f(t)\sin(\omega t)\,dt}_{\text{Imaginary part}}
$$

The **magnitude** tells us how strongly frequency $\omega$ is present in the signal:

$$
|F(\omega)| = \sqrt{\operatorname{Re}^2 + \operatorname{Im}^2}
$$

## Explore it

Pick a signal, choose a frequency $\omega$, and watch the decomposition in real time.
The **shaded areas** in the two plots _are_ the integrals — the real and imaginary parts of $F(\omega)$.
When positive and negative areas cancel, that frequency is weak.
When they reinforce, that frequency is strong.

### What to try

- **cos(3t)** — Set $\omega = 3$ and watch the cosine plot lock in phase: all positive area, no cancellation. Slide $\omega$ away and watch the area shrink as positive and negative parts cancel out.

- **cos(2t) + 0.5·cos(7t)** — The spectrum reveals both frequencies. The peak at $\omega = 7$ is half the height of the one at $\omega = 2$, matching the 0.5 coefficient.

- **Gaussian pulse** — A narrow pulse in time produces a wide spectrum (and vice versa). This is the uncertainty principle at work. Try increasing T to see the spectrum converge.

- **Rectangular pulse** — Sharp edges create a sinc pattern with ripples that never fully die out. Compare this to the smooth Gaussian spectrum.

- **Decaying exponential** — No sharp edges means no ripples. The spectrum is a smooth Lorentzian bell curve.
