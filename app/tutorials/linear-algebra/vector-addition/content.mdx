# Vector addition, geometrically

A **vector** is just a list of numbers — but that framing throws away most of the
intuition. The numbers are coordinates, and coordinates describe a *displacement*:
"go right 3, go up 1." Vector addition is what happens when you do one
displacement after another.

Drag the colored circles below. Vector $\mathbf{a}$ is indigo, $\mathbf{b}$ is rose,
and their sum $\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}$ is the emerald arrow.

<VectorAdditionPlayground2D />

Notice the dashed lines. They're copies of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ moved to
the *tip* of the other vector. That's the whole rule:

> **To add two vectors, lay them tip-to-tail. The sum is the arrow from the start of the first to the tip of the second.**

The two dashed lines plus the two solid ones make a parallelogram. This isn't a
coincidence — it's the same fact viewed two ways. You can think of $\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}$
as either:

1. Walk along $\mathbf{a}$, then walk along $\mathbf{b}$ (tip-to-tail), or
2. Walk along $\mathbf{b}$, then walk along $\mathbf{a}$.

You end up at the same place. That's why **vector addition is commutative**:

$$
\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{a}.
$$

## The component view

Geometrically, addition is "tip-to-tail." Algebraically, it's just adding
coordinates separately:

$$
\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}
= \begin{bmatrix} a_x \\\\ a_y \end{bmatrix}
+ \begin{bmatrix} b_x \\\\ b_y \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} a_x + b_x \\\\ a_y + b_y \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Both views are the same fact in different clothing. In code:

```ts
type Vec2 = { x: number; y: number };

function add(a: Vec2, b: Vec2): Vec2 {
  return { x: a.x + b.x, y: a.y + b.y };
}
```

That's it. The geometry justifies the formula; the formula generalizes to any
number of dimensions.

## In three dimensions

Everything you just learned is true in 3D, with one extra coordinate. The
parallelogram still exists — it's just tilted in space now. Orbit the camera by
dragging inside the canvas, and use the sliders to change each component.

Spin it around. Notice that the parallelogram is *flat* — it lies in a 2D plane
inside 3D space. Two vectors always span a plane (unless they're parallel, in
which case they span a line). That's a foreshadowing of a much bigger idea: the
**span** of a set of vectors is the space you can reach by adding scaled copies
of them. We'll get there.

## What this is teaching you

If you took only one thing away, it should be this: **a vector is a movement, and
adding vectors is just doing one movement after another.** Every other operation
in linear algebra — scaling, dot products, matrix multiplication, projections —
ultimately cashes out into "what does this *do* to a vector?" Holding on to the
geometric picture is what keeps the algebra honest.
