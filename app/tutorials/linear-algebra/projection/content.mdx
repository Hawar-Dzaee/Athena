# Projection, geometrically

Imagine shining a flashlight straight down onto a line. The shadow of a vector
on that line is its **projection**. Projection answers the question: *how much of
one vector goes in the direction of another?*

Drag the tips of $\mathbf{a}$ (indigo) and $\mathbf{b}$ (rose) below. The
emerald arrow is the projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$, and the
amber arrow is the **residual** — the leftover part.

<ProjectionPlayground2D />

Notice the right-angle marker where the residual meets $\mathbf{b}$. That's the
whole point: projection splits $\mathbf{a}$ into two pieces — one that lies
*along* $\mathbf{b}$ and one that's *perpendicular* to it.

## The formula

The projection of $\mathbf{a}$ onto $\mathbf{b}$ is:

$$
\text{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a})
= \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b}} \, \mathbf{b}
$$

Let's unpack this piece by piece:

1. **$\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}$** — the dot product measures how much $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ "agree" in direction.
2. **$\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{b}\|^2$** — we divide by the squared length of $\mathbf{b}$ to normalize.
3. **The ratio $\frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b}}$** — this is a *scalar* that tells you how many copies of $\mathbf{b}$ you need to reach the shadow.
4. **Multiply by $\mathbf{b}$** — scale $\mathbf{b}$ by that amount to get the actual projected vector.

If $\mathbf{b}$ is a **unit vector** ($\|\mathbf{b}\| = 1$), the formula simplifies to:

$$
\text{proj}_{\hat{\mathbf{b}}}(\mathbf{a}) = (\mathbf{a} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}}) \, \hat{\mathbf{b}}
$$

because $\hat{\mathbf{b}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}} = 1$.

## Why the right angle?

The **residual** (or error) vector is:

$$
\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a} - \text{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a})
$$

We can verify it's perpendicular to $\mathbf{b}$ by taking the dot product:

$$
\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{b}
= \left(\mathbf{a} - \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b}} \mathbf{b}\right) \cdot \mathbf{b}
= \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} - \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b}} (\mathbf{b} \cdot \mathbf{b})
= 0
$$

Zero. Always. This is the defining property of projection: among all
vectors on the line through $\mathbf{b}$, the projection is the one that makes
the residual shortest — the *closest point* on the line to the tip of
$\mathbf{a}$.

## The component view

In coordinates, with $\mathbf{a} = (a_x, a_y)$ and $\mathbf{b} = (b_x, b_y)$:

$$
\text{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{a})
= \frac{a_x b_x + a_y b_y}{b_x^2 + b_y^2}
\begin{bmatrix} b_x \\\\ b_y \end{bmatrix}
$$

In code:

```ts
type Vec2 = { x: number; y: number };

function dot(a: Vec2, b: Vec2): number {
  return a.x * b.x + a.y * b.y;
}

function proj(a: Vec2, b: Vec2): Vec2 {
  const scalar = dot(a, b) / dot(b, b);
  return { x: scalar * b.x, y: scalar * b.y };
}
```

## Where projection shows up

Projection isn't just a textbook exercise. It's a load-bearing operation in
applied math and ML:

- **Least squares regression** — fitting a line through data is projecting the
  target vector onto the column space of the design matrix.
- **Gram–Schmidt** — building an orthogonal basis works by repeatedly
  projecting out the components you've already accounted for.
- **PCA** — principal component analysis projects data onto the directions
  of maximum variance.
- **Attention in Transformers** — query–key dot products are projections that
  decide which values to attend to.

In every case, the idea is the same: decompose a vector into a part you
care about and a part you don't.

## What this is teaching you

Projection is the first operation in linear algebra where the **dot product
stops being just a number** and starts being a geometric tool. The dot product
tells you "how much of $\mathbf{a}$ is in the $\mathbf{b}$ direction" — and
projection makes that vague statement precise.
