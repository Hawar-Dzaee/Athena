# The determinant, geometrically

You have a $2 \times 2$ matrix. Its two columns are vectors. Those vectors span a
**parallelogram**. The determinant of the matrix is the **signed area** of that
parallelogram.

That's the whole idea. Everything else — the formula, the sign, the "singular" case —
follows from this single picture.

Try a few things:

- **Make the columns perpendicular** (e.g. $\text{col}_1 = (3, 0)$, $\text{col}_2 = (0, 2)$).
  The parallelogram becomes a rectangle, and the determinant is just width × height.
- **Make the columns parallel.** The parallelogram collapses to a line — area zero.
  This is why a matrix with parallel columns has no inverse: it squashes 2D space flat.
- **Swap the two columns.** The determinant flips sign. The parallelogram has the same
  shape, but the orientation — the direction you traverse its boundary — reverses.

the parallelogram is what $A$ *turns it into*. The determinant tells you the scaling
factor for area: if $|\det(A)| = 5$, the matrix makes every region five times larger.

## The formula

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\\\ c & d \end{bmatrix},
$$

the determinant is:

$$
\det(A) = ad - bc.
$$

Where does $ad - bc$ come from? The parallelogram spanned by
$(a, c)$ and $(b, d)$ has area equal to the absolute value of
$ad - bc$. You can verify this by computing the area of the bounding
rectangle and subtracting the triangles and rectangles outside the
parallelogram — the algebra always simplifies to $|ad - bc|$.

In code:

```ts
type Mat2 = { a: number; b: number; c: number; d: number };

function det(m: Mat2): number {
  return m.a * m.d - m.b * m.c;
}
```

## What the sign means

The determinant isn't just an area — it's a **signed** area. The sign records
**orientation**: the order in which the columns sweep around the origin.

- **Positive determinant:** column 1 → column 2 goes counterclockwise. The matrix
  preserves the "handedness" of shapes — if you draw a clockwise loop, its image
  under $A$ is still clockwise.
- **Negative determinant:** column 1 → column 2 goes clockwise. The matrix **reflects**
  — it flips left and right. A clockwise loop becomes counterclockwise.
- **Zero determinant:** the columns are parallel. The matrix **collapses** a dimension.
  No area, no inverse, no going back. This is the definition of a **singular** matrix.

Drag the vectors in the demo above until the determinant crosses zero. Watch the
parallelogram flatten into a line at the exact moment the sign flips. That transition —
from invertible to singular — is one of the most important thresholds in linear algebra.

## Determinant as a scaling factor

Here is another way to think about it. A matrix $A$ is a function that takes vectors
in and sends vectors out. It transforms the entire plane. The determinant tells you:

> **By what factor does $A$ scale areas, and does it flip orientation?**

If $\det(A) = 3$, every shape's area triples. If $\det(A) = -2$, every area doubles
*and* the shape gets mirrored. If $\det(A) = 0$, every shape gets flattened to a
lower dimension — all of 2D maps onto a line (or a point).

This extends to higher dimensions. For a $3 \times 3$ matrix, the three columns span
a **parallelepiped**, and the determinant is its signed volume. For $n \times n$, it's
the signed $n$-dimensional volume of the parallelotope spanned by the columns.

## Key properties

- **Multiplicative:** $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$. Applying $A$ then $B$ scales
  areas by both factors.
- **Transpose:** $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$. Rows carry the same geometric information
  as columns.
- **Inverse:** $\det(A^{-1}) = 1 / \det(A)$. Undoing a ×3 area scaling is a ×⅓
  scaling.
- **Swapping two columns** (or rows) flips the sign.
- **Scaling one column by $k$** multiplies the determinant by $k$.

These aren't arbitrary rules — they're all consequences of "the determinant is signed
area." Swapping columns reverses orientation; scaling a column stretches the
parallelogram along that edge.

## What this is teaching you

The determinant answers a single question: **what does this matrix do to area (or
volume)?** The formula $ad - bc$ is a shortcut to compute the answer, but the
*meaning* is geometric. Hold on to the parallelogram picture and the properties
above will never feel like memorisation.
