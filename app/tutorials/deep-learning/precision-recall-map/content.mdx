# Precision, Recall, and mAP

## The threshold tradeoff

Most models output a **confidence score** between 0 and 1 rather than a hard yes/no.
You choose a **threshold** $\tau$: predictions above it count as positive, below it as
negative.

- **High $\tau$**: only the most confident predictions make it through → fewer FPs →
  higher precision, but more real targets are missed → lower recall.
- **Low $\tau$**: almost everything is predicted positive → catches more real targets →
  higher recall, but floods in false alarms → lower precision.

## The precision-recall curve

Instead of committing to one threshold, sweep $\tau$ from 1 down to 0 and record the
$(recall, precision)$ pair at each step. The result is the **precision-recall curve** —
a complete picture of the classifier's tradeoff spectrum.

The curve always starts near the top-left: at a very high threshold only the model's
most confident (and usually correct) predictions are included, so precision is high but
recall is low. As you lower $\tau$ you catch more true positives (recall rises) while
also letting in more false alarms (precision generally falls). A **better classifier**
produces a curve that stays close to the top-right corner — high precision *at* high
recall. A random classifier hugs a flat horizontal line at the base rate.

## Average Precision (AP)

Comparing two classifiers by their curves is awkward — the curves can cross. **Average
Precision** collapses the whole curve into a single number: the area under it.

$$
\text{AP} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \bigl(r_k - r_{k-1}\bigr) \cdot P_k
$$

where $N$ is the total number of predictions, $r_k$ is the recall after the $k$-th
prediction is included, and $P_k$ is the precision at that point. Each term is a
rectangle of width $\Delta r_k$ and height $P_k$ — summing them gives the area under
the curve. AP lies in $[0, 1]$.

### Worked example

Suppose there are **5 actual positives** and your model ranks 8 predictions by
confidence score (highest first):

| Rank | Confidence | Label | Running TP | Running FP | Precision $P_k$ | Recall $r_k$ |
| ---- | :--------: | :---: | :--------: | :--------: | :-------------: | :----------: |
| 1    | 0.97       | ✓ TP  | 1          | 0          | 1.00            | 0.20         |
| 2    | 0.88       | ✓ TP  | 2          | 0          | 1.00            | 0.40         |
| 3    | 0.73       | ✗ FP  | 2          | 1          | 0.67            | 0.40         |
| 4    | 0.61       | ✓ TP  | 3          | 1          | 0.75            | 0.60         |
| 5    | 0.50       | ✗ FP  | 3          | 2          | 0.60            | 0.60         |
| 6    | 0.37       | ✓ TP  | 4          | 2          | 0.67            | 0.80         |
| 7    | 0.25       | ✗ FP  | 4          | 3          | 0.57            | 0.80         |
| 8    | 0.12       | ✓ TP  | 5          | 3          | 0.63            | 1.00         |

Now apply the summation. Only TPs advance recall ($\Delta r > 0$), so FPs contribute
zero directly — but they erode precision for the next TP:

| Rank | $r_{k-1}$ | $r_k$ | $\Delta r_k$ | $P_k$ | $\Delta r_k \cdot P_k$ |
| ---- | :-------: | :---: | :----------: | :---: | :--------------------: |
| 1 (TP) | 0.00 | 0.20 | **0.20** | 1.00 | 0.200 |
| 2 (TP) | 0.20 | 0.40 | **0.20** | 1.00 | 0.200 |
| 3 (FP) | 0.40 | 0.40 | **0.00** | 0.67 | 0.000 |
| 4 (TP) | 0.40 | 0.60 | **0.20** | 0.75 | 0.150 |
| 5 (FP) | 0.60 | 0.60 | **0.00** | 0.60 | 0.000 |
| 6 (TP) | 0.60 | 0.80 | **0.20** | 0.67 | 0.133 |
| 7 (FP) | 0.80 | 0.80 | **0.00** | 0.57 | 0.000 |
| 8 (TP) | 0.80 | 1.00 | **0.20** | 0.63 | 0.125 |

$$
\text{AP} = 0.200 + 0.200 + 0 + 0.150 + 0 + 0.133 + 0 + 0.125 = \mathbf{0.808}
$$

Notice what the three FPs do: they add nothing to AP themselves, but each one
increases the denominator $TP + FP$, so the next TP's precision is lower than it would
have been. A classifier that surfaces all five TPs *before* any FP would score
AP = 1.0.

## Mean Average Precision (mAP)

Real benchmarks have **multiple classes** — person, car, dog, and so on. Each class
gets its own PR curve and its own AP. **mAP** is simply the mean:

$$
\text{mAP} = \frac{1}{C} \sum_{c=1}^{C} \text{AP}_c
$$

A class that is visually ambiguous, underrepresented in training, or easily confused
with neighbours will have a low AP, pulling the overall mAP down.

## mAP@0.5 and COCO mAP

In **object detection** a predicted bounding box only counts as a TP if its
Intersection over Union (IoU) with the matching ground-truth box exceeds a threshold:

$$
\text{IoU} = \frac{\text{area of overlap}}{\text{area of union}}
$$

**Pascal VOC** uses IoU $\geq 0.50$ — often written **mAP@0.5**. The stricter
**COCO** metric averages mAP over ten IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05:

$$
\text{AP}_{\text{COCO}} = \frac{1}{10} \sum_{t \,\in\, \{0.50,\, 0.55,\, \ldots,\, 0.95\}} \text{mAP}@t
$$

This rewards detectors that localise boxes precisely — not just ones that drape a large
box over the right region and call it a day.

## Key takeaways

- **Accuracy** hides class-imbalance failures; precision and recall do not.
- The **threshold** $\tau$ controls the tradeoff: there is no free lunch between
  precision and recall.
- **AP** summarises the full precision-recall curve as a single area — the larger the
  better.
- **mAP** averages AP across classes; a weak class drags the whole score down.
- **COCO mAP** further averages over IoU thresholds, penalising sloppy box localisation.
